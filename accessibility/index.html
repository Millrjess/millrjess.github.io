Why Accessibility and UX Matter

What this page covers, in 30 seconds

Accessibility helps people use your website to find information, contact you, book a service, or complete a purchase, including people who use assistive technology. This page explains the standards I work with, how legal requirements differ by organization, and what accessibility testing can and cannot establish.

An accessible website reduces barriers for people with disabilities, including people who use keyboards, screen readers, magnification, or captions. Accessible pages are also easier for search engines to read, and they reduce legal and procurement risk. Usability, search, and risk are why Section 508, WCAG, and W3C validation are treated together, not as separate checklists.

What each term means

WCAG 2.2

WCAG stands for Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. It is the technical standard published by the World Wide Web Consortium, known as W3C, founded at MIT in 1994. WCAG 2.2, published in October 2023, organizes its requirements around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Most organizations target Level AA. It includes all Level A criteria plus the Level AA criteria, 55 criteria in total.

Section 508

Section 508 is a provision of United States federal law, part of the Rehabilitation Act. It requires federal agencies to make their electronic and information technology accessible to people with disabilities. Section 508 is not a separate web standard. Its revised standards, published in January 2017 with compliance required by January 18, 2018, incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level A and AA by reference for web content. In practice, a site that meets WCAG 2.2 AA and has valid parsing also meets the WCAG 2.0 AA baseline Section 508 incorporates, since WCAG 2.2 removed criterion 4.1.1, Parsing, which WCAG 2.0 still contains.

W3C validation

W3C validation checks whether the code of a page follows the published HTML standard. Valid code is not the same as an accessible page, and an accessible page can still have a validator warning. Validation matters because broken or ambiguous code is harder for browsers, assistive tools, and search engines to parse consistently.

Who has to comply

Deadlines and who they cover

Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act sets deadlines for state and local governments. Their web content and mobile apps must meet WCAG 2.1 AA by April 26, 2027, for entities serving 50,000 or more people, and April 26, 2028, for smaller entities, including special district governments. Private businesses open to the public fall under ADA Title III. There is no federal rule naming a WCAG version or a deadline for private businesses, but the Department of Justice has long taken the position that the ADA applies to goods and services offered online, and WCAG is the benchmark courts and settlements usually point to. Federal agencies, and vendors who supply them, are covered by Section 508 through procurement.

Why it matters for visitors and for business

For visitors

Accessibility is not only about compliance. A visitor who cannot use a keyboard, read low-contrast text, or follow a video without captions cannot complete a booking, a purchase, or a contact form. Fixing those barriers widens the audience that can actually use the site. Many accessibility improvements also make common tasks clearer and easier for other visitors. Clear headings, plain language, labeled forms, and buttons large enough to tap help a visitor in a hurry, on a small screen, or with a slow connection, as well as a visitor using assistive technology.

More than one in four US adults has a disability, according to the CDC. Vision disability alone affects 5.5% of US adults.

For the business

It also protects the business. An inaccessible selling page, booking flow, or checkout is a lost sale and a complaint risk at the same time. The same work that makes a page usable also makes the offer easier to understand.

The disability consumer market is worth $675 billion, according to Disability:IN. Yet 95.9% of the web's top million home pages fail basic accessibility checks, according to WebAIM. Federal website lawsuits rose 27% in 2025, to 3,117, according to Seyfarth Shaw.

Fewer dead ends and abandoned forms mean fewer lost inquiries and support messages asking what a page means.

For procurement

For businesses that sell to government, schools, or large organizations, accessibility also affects procurement. Those buyers often require WCAG conformance in a contract, even when a private business is not directly covered by Section 508 or ADA Title II. A site that already meets WCAG 2.2 AA is easier to put forward in that process than a site that would need to be rebuilt first.

How it helps rankings, without overstating it

Accessibility is not a named Google ranking factor, and passing an accessibility audit does not by itself lift a page in search results. Accessibility and technical SEO overlap in practices such as descriptive headings, meaningful link text, text alternatives, and clear page structure. That overlap does not make accessibility conformance a ranking guarantee. Accessibility work should be evaluated primarily by whether it removes barriers for people.
