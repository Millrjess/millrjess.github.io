Books by Jessica A. Miller

The Ledger Series

A trilogy about a civic system that manages its citizens’ memories the way other systems manage traffic: efficiently, with appropriate authority, without malice.

Find the trilogy on Amazon

The Ledger Series is a trilogy about a civic system that manages its citizens' memories the way other systems manage traffic: efficiently, with appropriate authority, without malice.

Book One: a reviewer in the reconciliation division reads a file she was never assigned. Book Two: She discovers the counter-signal bears her own design signature. Book Three: She is the seventy-fourth version of herself to try to stop it.

Whether any of them succeed is a question about what success means when the self doing the succeeding may not be the self that started.

Book One: The Second Account

Eleven years. One city that has learned to smooth away anything it cannot manage.

Aura Voss is a reviewer in the reconciliation division. Her job is to review discrepancy reports: citizens who remember things the official record shows never happened. People who insist on a spouse the registry doesn't recognize. A child who says she saw birds somewhere where birds no longer exist.

Aura's job is to approve the corrections. For eleven years, she has been very good at it.

When a dark-red case file lands in her queue by apparent error, she reads it. It concerns a man who can describe a wedding the system has no record of, down to the weather and the tear in a coat cuff that was never mended. She notes that she took no action.

This is not true.

The Second Account is the first book in The Ledger Series: a trilogy about what a system does to the self when the self has agreed to look away long enough.

For readers of Kazuo Ishiguro, Yoko Ogawa, and Emily St. John Mandel.

Book Two: The Echo Trace

The Ledger did not collapse. It learned.

Three weeks after the system's failure, Aura Voss is underground with a coat that has no tracking thread and a list of things the city no longer agrees happened. The Ledger has rebuilt itself around the interruption she caused, smarter for having faced it. The reassertion arrives every morning. She catalogs the texture of what it takes.

In the unmanaged sector beyond the city's map, she finds what the system has been maintaining at the cost of everything else: a pharmaceutical manifest reclassifying four hundred elderly residents as negative efficiency ratios. A settlement that breathes unmonitored air. Her father, sixty feet underground, who has been building the only thing that can end this.

And a counter-signal bearing her own design signature.

The Echo Trace is the second book in The Ledger Series. This series asks not what the system did to its citizens, but what the citizens agreed to do on its behalf.

"The system did not call this a death sentence. The system called it pathway optimization."

For readers of Kazuo Ishiguro, Yoko Ogawa, and Emily St. John Mandel.

Book Three: The Ghost Index

The first two books of The Ledger Series are narrated by Aura Voss in the first person. This one is not.

By the time this story begins, Aura has been suppressed seventy-three times in twenty-three years. The Ledger took what it decided she did not need to keep, reset her to baseline, and returned her to the registry. The woman who walks out of the mountain at the start of this book has made it further than every previous version of herself.

In the Ghost Index, a hidden repository below the city's transit infrastructure, she will find the files of the seventy-three iterations who came before her: each attempt, each failure, and each thing the system invented specifically to stop her. Iteration 31 made it react. Iteration 59 used her last four seconds not to fight what was happening, but to write a sentence for a version of herself she would never meet.

Whether the woman reading that sentence is still the person who can say I is what this book is about.

The Ghost Index is the conclusion of The Ledger Series. It ends where the series always intended to end: with a system that has been answering for twenty-three years receiving no answer.
